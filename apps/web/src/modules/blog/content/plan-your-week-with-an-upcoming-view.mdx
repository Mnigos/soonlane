Most to-do lists are piles. You add things as they come up, and the list grows from the top or the bottom. That works for capturing tasks. It does not tell you which day you will do them on, and that is what planning a week means.

A plan for the week is a list with a day attached to every task. Monday has these four things, Tuesday has these two, Friday is light because of the dentist. When each task sits under a day, you can see at a glance which days are full and which are empty, and you fix that before the week starts instead of on Thursday night.

This is the routine I use every Sunday evening. It takes about 15 minutes. I use Soonlane's Upcoming view for it, because that view is a day-by-day list, but the steps work in any app that sorts tasks by date.

## Why plan by day

A task without a day never comes due. "Renew passport photos" can sit on a list for a month, because no day ever asks for it. Give it Wednesday and on Wednesday it is one of five things, not one of fifty.

Days also show you the size of your week. Ten tasks on one list look manageable. The same ten tasks spread over a calendar week show you that Tuesday has six of them and you are out all afternoon.

## The weekly routine

### 1. Clear what slipped

Start with last week's leftovers. Every task that is still open and past its date is overdue. Decide for each one: do it this week, move it further out, or delete it. If the pile is big, [what to do with overdue tasks](/blog/what-to-do-with-overdue-tasks) goes through it in more detail.

Do this first. Overdue tasks you skip now come back on Monday morning and push out what you planned.

### 2. Put down the fixed things

Some tasks only make sense at one time: a call at 10:30, a run at 18:00, a bill due on the 14th. Give them their day first, and a due time when they have one. They are the parts of the week you cannot move, so everything else fits around them.

### 3. Empty your head into the week

Now write down everything else you want to get done this week. Put each task under a day as you write it. Do not sort first and assign days later. Picking the day while the task is in front of you is faster, and you see the day fill up as you go.

When you cannot pick a day, the task is probably not for this week. Put it on a day next week or the week after, so it comes back when you plan again.

### 4. Look at the shape of the week

Scroll through the days once. Look for a day with twice as many tasks as the others, or a day packed around fixed appointments. Move a few tasks from the busy day to a quiet one. Leave one day lighter than the rest; something unplanned always turns up.

### 5. Pick the first task for Monday

Before you close the list, decide what you will do first on Monday. One task. On Monday morning you start with it instead of starting with the list.

## Doing it in Soonlane's Upcoming view

Upcoming is the view Soonlane opens on. It is one long scroll: Overdue at the top, then today, then tomorrow and every day after it, each under a heading like "13 Nov · Friday". It keeps loading more weeks as you scroll, so next month is there when you need it.

Above the list is the week strip, Monday to Sunday. It shows the week of the day at the top of the list, and today has its number on a red badge. Click a day in the strip and the list scrolls to it. Days before today are greyed out. Next to the "Upcoming" title are two arrows that step one day back or forward, and a button between them that names the day at the top of the list. Click that button to go back to today.

Here is how the routine maps onto it.

Clear what slipped. The Overdue section sits above today. Its Reschedule button moves every overdue task to one day you pick, so if all of last week's leftovers belong on Monday, that is one click and a date. To handle them one at a time, use the "…" menu on each task, or complete or delete them.

Put down the fixed things. Open the task with Edit, click its date, and set a time in the date picker. Inside a day, tasks with a time come first in time order, then the rest by priority from P1 to P4. Your appointments end up at the top of each day.

Empty your head into the week. Every day in Upcoming has its own "Add task" row. A task you add under Wednesday is due on Wednesday, and the composer stays open for the next one. Use the strip to jump between days instead of scrolling.

Look at the shape of the week. Each task's "…" menu has a Date row with Today, Tomorrow, Next week and, from Monday to Thursday, This weekend. "More dates" opens the full date picker, where you can type a date instead of clicking. The picker reads words like `tom`, `fri`, `next week` or `14 Nov`.

Pick the first task for Monday. Give it P1. The circle turns red and the task moves to the top of Monday among the tasks without a time.

## During the week

The weekly plan does not need daily replanning. It needs a two-minute look each morning. Soonlane's Today view shows only Overdue and today's tasks, with nothing from the rest of the week. Start there. When a task will not happen today, move it to the day it will happen, not just to tomorrow by default.

When you complete a task, it leaves the list at once, and a toast offers Undo in case you clicked the wrong circle.

## Tasks that come back every week

Weekly planning gets shorter when the tasks you do every week plan themselves. Bins on Tuesday, a review on Friday, groceries on Saturday. Give them a repeat rule once and they show up on the right day each week. [Repeating tasks](/blog/repeating-tasks) covers how the rules work and what happens when you complete one late.

## Where Soonlane fits

I built Soonlane as an [open source Todoist alternative](/) around this way of planning. It has no projects, and tasks without a date go to the Inbox. Upcoming is the main view, and labels are the only way to group tasks, so the whole week sits in one list. It is free, and you sign in with Google.
